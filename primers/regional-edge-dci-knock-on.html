Regional & Edge DCI: the AI-inference "knock-on" theme — a market deep-dive primer

Type: market deep-dive primer (descriptive / diagnostic). Audience: a smart finance professional (PM/analyst) oriented to nothing specific in optical networking; technical terms are defined parenthetically on first use. What you’ll be able to do after reading: tell, with evidence, whether the AI-inference “knock-on optical wave” at the edge is real today, which of its proposed mechanisms are operative versus aspirational, where the spend actually lands, and exactly which public and private companies capture which tier of it.

This primer is descriptive: it maps what is real today. It carries no forward projections, no forecast or scenario math, no model, and no buy/sell recommendation. Where a source offers a forward number it is tagged “(forecast)” and never built upon.

1. The question, and why it matters

The knock-on hypothesis is that hyperscaler AI capex, by pushing inference outward toward users, should ignite a secondary wave of optical and interconnect spend at edge data centers, content-delivery networks (CDNs), and regional/tier-2/tier-3 carriers and colocation providers — and the question this primer answers is whether that wave is measurable today and who captures it.

The hypothesis has named proponents. CBRE states plainly that the shift “from AI training to AI Inference demand is creating a need for more regional and distributed data centers, not just hyperscale hubs,” because inference “often need to be near end users” 1. Equinix, the largest carrier-neutral colocation operator, projects private interconnection capacity growing at a 29% compound annual growth rate (CAGR) through 2027 — to nearly 40,000 terabits per second (forecast) — and attributes the growth to “AI, edge computing and distributed ecosystem business models” 2. So the bull narrative is not a strawman: serious operators assert that inference is dispersing and that interconnect demand is rising with it.

The question is therefore not whether inference disperses — it does, at the margin — but whether that dispersion has yet produced measurable third-party optical spend at the edge tiers, and which companies capture the dollars it does move. This matters because the answer routes capital very differently depending on the tier. If the wave is genuinely dispersing to tier-3, the long tail of regional carriers, municipal networks and small colocation operators (and the sub-scale vendors that serve them) is where the incremental dollar lands. If it is not — if the measured money is hyperscaler campus-cluster and long-haul interconnect — then the dollar lands with scale systems vendors and component suppliers, and the tail captures only a fragmented residual.

This is the regional/edge counterpart to two sister deliverables that the reader should treat as already-read context. The scale-across DCI primer (vault/kb/outputs/primers/2026-06-11-dci-scale-across-enablement.md) covers the hyperscaler campus-cluster and long-haul interconnect build — the “scale-across” leg — and the bottom-up sizing of that market; this primer does not re-derive it. The Ribbon/ADTRAN DC-networking report (vault/kb/outputs/primers/2026-06-16-rbbn-adtran-dc-networking.md) covers the two named sub-scale vendors at company depth; this primer treats them as roster entries within a tiered framework rather than re-explaining their segment mechanics. Defined terms used throughout: DCI = data-center interconnect (the optical links between data centers); DWDM = dense wavelength-division multiplexing (carrying many “colors” of light on one fiber to multiply capacity); coherent pluggable = a small modular coherent optical transceiver that plugs directly into a switch or router port; ZR/ZR+ optics = the OIF-standardized coherent pluggable types, ZR reaching ~120 km and ZR+ reaching ROADM-network distances; IPoDWDM = IP-over-DWDM, terminating the optical wavelength inside the router instead of a separate transponder box; ROADM = reconfigurable optical add-drop multiplexer (the device that routes wavelengths in a mesh network); neocloud = a GPU-centric specialist cloud; MOFN = manage-on-others’-network (a hyperscaler contracting a carrier to build for it); KV-cache = the key-value attention cache that holds prior tokens during inference; CPO = co-packaged optics (optics integrated next to the chip rather than at the faceplate).

2. The state of the debate: the bull narrative versus what the independent data shows

The bull case is that an outward-dispersing inference workload spawns a secondary optical wave at the edge; the independent data shows the opposite where the bull needs it most — the optical wave is real but concentrated in hyperscaler scale-across, while the segment geographically nearest the edge is the weakest part of the market.

Start with the cleanest disconfirming anchor: no independent analyst house sizes “edge/regional-inference optical spend” as a discrete line at all. Dell’Oro’s total optical-transport equipment market reached $16B in 2025 (+10% year-over-year), and the recovery was “driven by demand for data center interconnect” — with direct purchases of WDM equipment for DCI up nearly 40% and direct cloud-provider purchases up about 50%, the top three suppliers being Ciena, Nokia and Cisco, and “high concentration of revenue coming from North America” 33. Cignal AI’s optical-component market hit a record ~$25B in 2025 (datacom >$18B, telecom coherent modules ~$6B) 4. Both are real, large, and growing — and neither breaks out an edge or regional-inference line. Dell’Oro’s VP is explicit that even the fast-growing neocloud buyer sits inside the cloud/DCI aggregate and is not separately sized 5. The knock-on is asserted by vendors; it is never sized by an analyst. That absence is the first finding, not a research gap.

The second finding is that the boom and the edge are not the same thing — and the two can both be true. The same Dell’Oro coverage that reports DCI +40% 3 also reports that WDM Metro — the optical segment geographically closest to edge/regional inference — declined for a seventh consecutive quarter through 1Q 2025, headwinded by IPoDWDM adoption, inventory glut and macro 6. And in the longer view, Dell’Oro finds that DWDM spending by “the rest of the market” (telecom and regional carriers — the tier-2/3 layer) was flat from 2017 to 2024, even as hyperscaler direct DWDM purchases grew at a 12% CAGR 7. There is no contradiction between “DCI is booming +40%” and “metro/regional is flat-to-declining,” because the boom is hyperscale campus-cluster and long-haul, not the metro/regional layer the edge thesis requires 38. This is the single most important reconciliation in the primer: the aggregate must never stand in for “the edge is booming.”

The third finding bounds the physics. Nokia reports that inter-facility (between-data-center) traffic is growing roughly 23% per year while traffic inside data centers is growing about 65% per year — and its largest fiber examples (up to 13,000 fibers between sites) are hyperscaler campus-to-campus 9. Intra-DC traffic outpaces inter-facility by about 3x, which tells us most AI bandwidth never leaves the building or campus. The bull case needs bandwidth to disperse outward; the bandwidth is instead piling up inside the campus.

So the debate resolves not into “bull versus bear” but into a more precise claim: a real, measured optical wave exists, it is hyperscaler scale-across, North-America-concentrated, and the layer the edge thesis depends on is the weakest part of that wave. The rest of the primer establishes which mechanisms are live, where the spend lands, who the buyers are, and who captures the dollar by tier.

3. The evidence, by sub-claim

3a — Demand and sizing reality

The reusable lens for the rest of this primer is four named tiers of buyer — hyperscaler, neocloud, long-haul/regional carrier, and the regional/metro/municipal tail — because the capture answer is different at each, and the measured demand is overwhelmingly in the first and third.

The cleanest sizing anchor is the one already stated: $16B total optical transport, DCI-driven, +40% on the DCI line, NA-concentrated, no edge sub-line 335. To that, add the component view ($25B, coherent modules ~$6B) 4, which confirms the wave is real at the silicon/optics layer too. The honest read is that the demand is large and growing but is measured at the wrong granularity for the thesis: every published number aggregates the edge into the cloud/DCI bucket.

The proxy that matters most is the “rest of market flat” datapoint 7: hyperscaler DWDM spend was ~$2B in 2024 (heading to >$3B by 2029, forecast) growing at a 12% CAGR, while non-hyperscaler DWDM — telecom and regional carriers, i.e. the edge tiers — was flat over seven years. The tier-2/3 layer’s aggregate optical spend is not merely unsized; where it can be proxied, it is flat. Hold this against the WDM-Metro seven-quarter decline 6, and the demand-side verdict is settled: the edge tiers are not yet pulling optical equipment revenue up.

3b — The mechanism map: which of five drivers is operative-today versus aspirational

Five mechanisms could plausibly push optical spend outward — latency-sensitive inference, data sovereignty, CDN refresh, metro/middle-mile capacity, and edge KV-caching — but only the first is demonstrably operative today as an outward push, and even it rides existing fiber plus new GPUs rather than new optical orders.

Latency-sensitive inference is live. The cleanest operative-today anchor is Akamai Inference Cloud, a generally-available platform launched October 2025 that extends inference from core data centers to the edge using NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs and BlueField-3 DPUs, targeting 20 initial launch locations 10. Cloudflare runs a unified edge-inference layer across 330 cities with sub-50ms latency on NVIDIA L4/T4 GPUs, explicitly to process inference at a nearby edge DC rather than a distant cloud region 11. Both are real today — and both run on the operators’ own networks. Crucially, the spend lands on GPUs and DPUs at existing points of presence: Akamai’s AI Grid handles networking via BlueField DPUs, not new optical transport 12, and Charter/Spectrum’s edge build runs over its own 100 Gbps fiber, an upgrade of in-place plant rather than a greenfield long-haul order 13. The mechanism is operative; its optical knock-on is utilization of existing fiber, not third-party metro-DWDM orders.

The other four candidate mechanisms are aspirational, intra-cluster, or treated elsewhere as regional-optical drivers (metro/middle-mile capacity, the fifth, is taken up in §3c). On data sovereignty, the corpus offers no evidence of it driving optical purchases today: the nearest datapoint is Akamai expanding compute into “major metros that lack cloud computing options” 14 — an edge-footprint fact, not a sovereignty-driven optical buy — so sovereignty remains aspirational as an optical driver. Edge KV-caching is the most technical of the candidates, and the corpus places it firmly inside the cluster: KV-cache and model-cache movement occurs over RDMA / InfiniBand / NVLink (NVIDIA’s NIXL transfer engine, Mooncake) between prefill and decode nodes, with no primary tying it to inter-regional optical buy 15. CDN refresh is real as a product (the Akamai/Cloudflare layers above) but its optical implication is unquantified. So the mechanism map collapses from five candidates to one live driver — and that one is GPU-led.

This forces a careful treatment of the most-quoted edge number in the theme. Akamai is widely cited as deploying GPUs into “more than 4,400 edge locations” — but that figure is a claimed footprint, not proven inference-at-scale. The 4,400 figure is Akamai’s own claim, from its AI Grid release 12; the skeptical reaction is a reader’s — Om Malik (“When I read that Akamai is putting NVIDIA Blackwell-edition GPUs into more than 4,400 edge locations, I chuckled”) 16; it is a deployment count whose optical-dollar implication is explicitly unquantified 12; and Akamai’s own 10-K reports ~4,300 edge points of presence as a CDN footprint into which it buys bandwidth and colocation, not one from which it captures vendor optical margin 17. The operative-today anchor for edge inference is therefore the 20-site Akamai Inference Cloud 10 and Cloudflare’s 330-city layer 11 — the 4,400 figure belongs in the record only as a footprint claim, never as evidence that inference-at-the-edge is at scale.

3c — The knock-on chain and where spend actually lands

The well-evidenced money does not flow to tier-3 edge inference; it flows along a different path — campus-to-campus DCI and long-haul dark fiber for the hyperscaler and neocloud core — and the real 2026 “regional” transport wave is hub-to-metro long-haul captured by scale carriers.

The cleanest dollar anchors on this path are large and named. Lumen has closed nearly $13B in Private Connectivity Fabric deals with hyperscalers, neoclouds, social platforms and AI companies, scaling toward 47 million intercity fiber miles by 2028 (forecast) 18. Zayo booked more than $1B in AI-related long-haul deals in 2024 with a further ~$3B in pipeline, as hyperscaler orders shifted from 8–12 fibers per route pre-AI to 12–48 fiber pairs (some 144–432) 19. Where this build is “regional,” it is hub-to-metro long-haul, not edge dispersion: Zayo’s 8,000-route-mile AI-corridor program concentrates in “a defined set of high-value corridors” connecting power-rich data centers to adjacent metros 20, and the Midco/Switch deal lights 500-plus 400 Gbps circuits (200 Tb capacity) on diverse routes from Ellendale, North Dakota to Chicago — a remote power-sited DC backhauled to a hub metro, with Nokia (a scale vendor) on the parallel route 21. The knock-on chain is real and produces real 2026 spend, but its shape is power-sited-DC → long-haul/metro hub, captured by scale carriers — a different animal from inference-at-the-edge.

This is also how the Equinix interconnect-growth claim reconciles with the “training stays co-located” view. Equinix’s 29% interconnection CAGR (forecast) cites edge 2, but Equinix is itself a scale interconnection landlord reporting $1.6B in annualized gross bookings (+27%), and (separately) its Fiber Connect provides dark-fiber links between data centers 2, and Deloitte frames distributed and smaller inferencing data centers as increasingly able to handle the load and “will likely” emerge — forward-looking language, not a present-tense deployment 22. The growing interconnect bandwidth is east-west DCI between scale facilities, not last-mile edge. There is no contradiction to resolve once the granularity is fixed: Equinix’s number is real and it is a scale-facility number.

3d — The buyer universe

The buyer universe for regional/edge optical is genuinely large and deeply fragmented — and that fragmentation is precisely why no source can isolate its optical spend; the dollar disappears into the cloud/DCI aggregate.

The counts are substantial. There are roughly 190–200 distinct neocloud operators worldwide, of which only ~10–15 operate at meaningful scale in the US (JLL/RCR and GlobalData, late 2025 — ranges retained because the firms and dates differ) 23, collectively doing more than $5B of revenue in Q2 2025 (+205% year-over-year) 24. The US colocation market spans ~2,226 colocation businesses (IBISWorld 2025), with Equinix and Digital Realty holding under 21% of 2024 revenue combined, leaving ~80% to a long tail of regional and tier-2/3 operators 25. There are 1,561 active US fiber providers as of FCC BDC Version 7 26, and the CDN/edge fleets are large (Akamai >4,000 nodes, Cloudflare 330+ cities, CloudFront >600 embedded PoPs) 27. Yet Dell’Oro’s VP confirms the gap directly: neocloud and tier-2/3 colo optical spend sits inside the cloud/DCI aggregate and is not broken out as a standalone figure 5. The buyer pool is deep and numerous; its optical-spend line does not exist in published research. The closest proxies are the flat “rest of market” DWDM datapoint 7 and a ~$2B long-haul wavelength services market in which Lumen and Zayo hold ~90% 28 — both of which point the same way: where the edge tiers can be measured, the dollars are concentrated upward.

One buyer-side wrinkle reshapes the whole picture: the fastest-growing emerging buyer is being courted by a scale vendor. Ciena reports it is engaged with about two dozen neoscalers (CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda among them) that “have already decided that they need to build out their own optical network infrastructure” 29. Ciena’s own framing puts $8–10B of a ~$50B-by-2029 TAM (forecast) in “scale-across” in-and-around-the-DC and more than $20B in long-haul/metro 30, with its first multi-rail hyperscaler award rolling out from 2027 (forecast) 30. The neocloud DCI dollar — the highest-growth slice of the emerging buyer base — leaks up to scale, not down to sub-scale.

4. Who captures it — the tiered capture framework

The capture answer is the report’s centerpiece, and it is tiered: scale systems vendors and component players take the money tiers (hyperscaler, neocloud, long-haul), while non-scale vendors reliably take only the fragmented regional/metro/municipal tail — with a sharp ceiling of zero non-scale wins at the hyperscaler or marquee-neocloud tier.

4a — The money tiers: scale systems and components

Scale systems. The quantitative spine of the capture story is that Ciena’s Optical Networking line alone was $3.25B in FY2025, up 22.8% 42 — an order of magnitude larger than any single sub-scale optical franchise, and several times the entire sub-scale set combined — and it is growing far faster. the $453.4M Americas revenue jump was driven by demand from cloud providers and communications service providers, the segment in which Ciena’s Waveserver serves as the workhorse DCI platform 43; its top two customers (both cloud) account for about one-third of sales 30; and — most relevant to the thesis — Ciena names “next-generation metro and edge networking” as an explicit growth pillar, integrating IP routing with coherent optics and targeting metro DCI/access at 800G and 1.6T 4343. The regional/metro DCI socket the sub-scale vendors hold is exactly the addressable market Ciena names as its growth target, which means the leak vector is structural rather than incidental.

Consolidation reinforces it. Nokia completed its ~$2.3B acquisition of Infinera in February 2025, boosting its Optical Networks business by 75% and absorbing a mid-scale DCI/metro/line-systems vendor (one tier above the sub-scale names) into a scale player 44. And the architecture itself is collapsing the standalone box: Cisco/Acacia leads coherent pluggables, with more than 70% of coherent ports now pluggable, and its Routed Optical Networking (RON) drops a coherent pluggable directly into a switch or router — eliminating the standalone DWDM transponder where ADTRAN/ADVA, Ribbon and Ekinops sell 45. Dell’Oro confirms the top-3 DCI suppliers are Ciena, Nokia and Cisco 31. The structural shift moves the interconnect dollar from a dedicated optical system (sub-scale turf) into the router plus the pluggable module — both scale-captured.

Components. Beneath the systems vendors, the component layer captures the silicon and optics of DCI growth tier-agnostically — but overwhelmingly at hyperscale scale. Marvell’s data-center end market was $6.1B (74% of revenue) in FY2026, with optical interconnect roughly half of that and DCI modules slated for all five major hyperscalers in FY27 (forecast) 4632. Coherent’s Networking segment profit rose 82% to $644M, explicitly naming DCI demand 47. Lumentum supplies lasers, PICs and transceivers across access, metro, long-haul and submarine to the network-equipment makers 48. Fabrinet — the contract manufacturer behind the optical vendors — saw DCI revenue reach $197M (+90% year-over-year), its fastest-growing line, a clean real-today gauge that DCI optical is the fastest-growing optical category 49; Cignal puts coherent modules at ~$6B 4. Whoever wins the box, the component layer captures the DCI silicon — but the named growth is hyperscale, not tier-2/3.

A coverage note prevents a common conflation: the highest-growth interconnect names are intra-DC and orthogonal to the regional knock-on. Credo’s HiWire active electrical cables are rack/row copper for scale-up/scale-out 50, and Astera Labs’ $308.4M Q1 (+93%) PCIe/CXL retimers connect within and between servers 51. Both are inside-the-DC and touch no metro/regional optical transport. They are the highest-growth, highest-margin names in the interconnect universe — and irrelevant to the edge-DCI thesis. The reader who equates “AI interconnect boom” with “edge-DCI boom” is conflating two different markets.

4b — The tail: the repeatable non-scale capture pattern

The most important affirmative finding for the bottom tier is that non-scale capture is a repeatable pattern, not a one-off. The corpus holds eight named, real-today wins on non-scale vendors, each at the regional/metro/municipal tier: Smartoptics upgraded German regional ISP TKRZ’s backbone from 10G CWDM to 100G/400G open DWDM 33 and replaced rural-Illinois CSP Geneseo’s aging 10G core 34; Ekinops won Western-Kentucky municipal operator MuniNet’s 400G coherent transport 35 and the NW-Colorado Project THOR middle-mile network 36; ADTRAN won Belgian regional cloud provider Cegeka’s FSP 3000 DCI deployment to a new Brussels data center 37 and Italy’s wholesale-only FiberCop nationwide metro rollout 38; Ribbon won Planters Broadband’s 400G/800G regional build 39; and PacketLight interconnected five data centers across Singapore and Indonesia for an ASEAN regional service provider over 1,200 km 40. The recurring selling point is identical across all eight — “cost-effective,” and avoidance of Tier-1 vendor lock-in. That repetition is what turns the tail from anecdote into pattern: non-scale vendors reliably win regional ISPs, municipal/middle-mile networks and wholesale fiber operators.

But the tail is thin and uneven, and only one vendor is clearly monetizing it today. Smartoptics — an open-DWDM pure-play — grew FY2025 revenue 35.6% to $75.3M, with Q4 a record $23.2M (+37.7%), explicitly on “AI related DCI demand… new orders from major operators, neo-scalers, and Internet Content Providers across EMEA and the US” 41. Its closest comparable, Ekinops, ran the opposite way: optical revenue declined ~12% in FY2025 as two North American customers cut capex, and its dedicated DCI product line (PTM) does not commercialize until end-2026, with a vendor-self-estimated 13–20% DCI growth (a company forecast, not an independent market figure) 52. So even the cleanest non-scale comparable is forward-loaded; the present-tense capture is concentrated in the one vendor executing on open-DWDM.

And the ceiling is sharp: zero non-scale wins at the hyperscaler or marquee-neocloud tier. The neocloud tier is captured by scale player Ciena 29; Ciena’s book is concentrated among a small number of large customers 43; and Ciena’s optical line is several times the entire sub-scale set combined — an order of magnitude larger than any single sub-scale optical franchise 42. No corpus finding records a single non-scale win at the hyperscaler or marquee-neocloud tier. This boundary is what makes the framework genuinely tiered rather than a smooth gradient: the sub-scale vendors are structurally locked out of the money tiers and reliably present only in the fragmented tail.

4d — The Ribbon “67% rural DCI” stress-test

Ribbon markets a “Rural DCI” thesis asserting “67% of US DCI growth is in rural areas”; the correct treatment is to refute the wording and confirm the direction, because the number is a relabel of a facility count, and capacity-weighted data refutes the spend implication while confirming that secondary builds are a real growing tail.

Ribbon’s Q1-2026 earnings deck states, verbatim, “USA: 67% of DCI growth is in rural areas,” sourced to “Pew Research & Datacentermap.com” 66. But Pew’s underlying finding is that 67% of planned US data-center facilities (a count) are in rural areas — not 67% of DCI growth, spend or bandwidth 67. The relabel matters because a facility count and a capacity/spend share are different quantities: in Pew’s database a 10 MW edge site and a 1,000 MW hyperscale campus each count as one facility 67. 6. Synthesis — where the weight of evidence lands

The evidence-weighted verdict is that the edge-inference “knock-on optical wave” is real but tiered and largely not-yet-at-the-edge: the measured money is hyperscaler scale-across (campus plus long-haul, North-America-concentrated), captured by scale systems vendors and component players, while non-scale vendors reliably capture only the fragmented regional/metro/municipal tail.

Three findings carry the verdict, and they compound. First, the demand is unsized at the edge granularity and, where it can be proxied, the edge tiers are flat-to-declining: no analyst publishes an edge-optical line 5, WDM Metro declined for seven straight quarters 6, and the non-hyperscaler “rest of market” DWDM spend was flat 2017–2024 7. Second, only one of five candidate mechanisms is operative today as an outward push, and even latency-sensitive inference rides existing fiber plus new GPUs rather than new optical orders 101112; sovereignty as a sized optical driver and edge KV-caching are aspirational or intra-cluster 1415. Third, capture splits cleanly by tier: the money tiers leak to scale systems (Ciena $3.25B optical, +22.8% 42) and components (Marvell $6.1B data-center, 74% of revenue 46; Fabrinet DCI +90% 49), the fastest-growing emerging buyer (neoclouds) is captured by scale player Ciena 29, and non-scale vendors win a repeatable but bounded regional/metro tail — eight named wins 3334353637383940 — with zero wins at the hyperscaler or marquee-neocloud tier 2943.

The Ribbon “67% rural DCI” claim crystallizes the whole verdict in one number: the facility count genuinely tilts rural 67, but the DCI dollars stay metro-concentrated (Northern Virginia ~3.5x all secondary markets combined 68; top 20 metros = 62% of global hyperscale capacity 69). The regional opportunity is real and growing in direction 1 but small in magnitude relative to the hubs. The honest one-sentence answer to “is the edge-inference optical wave real, and who captures it?” is: a real optical wave exists, it is hyperscaler scale-across rather than edge dispersion, scale systems and components capture the money tiers, and the only reliable non-scale capture is the fragmented regional/metro/municipal tail — monetized today essentially by Smartoptics alone.

7. Investment lens (qualitative tie to John’s AI/HPC-services thesis)

This primer maps the regional/edge slice of the optical knock-on onto John’s AI/HPC-services thesis as a present-tense capture map, not a forecast — there is no model for this run and no cited model cells.

For the AI/HPC-services thesis, the theme resolves into a simple routing rule for where the optical knock-on dollar lands. At the money tiers — hyperscaler, neocloud, long-haul — the dollar is captured overwhelmingly by scale systems vendors and component players: Ciena’s $3.25B optical line (+22.8%) is an order of magnitude larger than any single sub-scale optical franchise — several times the entire sub-scale set combined — and growing faster 42, and the component layer (Marvell, Coherent, Lumentum, Fabrinet) captures the silicon tier-agnostically but concentrated at hyperscale 463249. The non-scale tail is monetizable — but only by the names executing on open-DWDM against a flat rest-of-market 7 and a structurally declining metro layer 6; in the present tense that is essentially Smartoptics (+35.6% on AI-DCI demand 41), with Ekinops’s comparable opportunity still forward-loaded to end-2026 52. The thesis read is that the regional/edge knock-on is a real but thin and bounded capture pool for non-scale vendors, sitting beneath a much larger scale-captured pool — and that the cleanest exposures to the edge-inference demand (Crusoe, EdgeConneX, DataBank, Vapor IO, Everstream, Segra) are private demand-origin and transport-layer names rather than the vendors that capture equipment margin. This is qualitative by design: with no model and no forecast permitted, the lens is a map of present-tense capture, not a sizing.

Counter-thesis (the strongest bull read): the dispersion is simply early, and edge inference WILL push optical spend out to tier-3 by 2027–28 — at which point the non-scale tail thickens and the “flat metro” diagnosis inverts. The receipts the bull points to are forward-loaded but named: Ciena’s first multi-rail hyperscaler award and its ~$50B-by-2029 TAM ramp from 2027 (forecast) 3030, Dell’Oro framing 100 km campus-cluster interconnect as the 2026 use case that normalizes regional coherent (forecast) 94, Deloitte’s expectation that smaller distributed inferencing data centers emerge 22, and CBRE’s observation that tertiary pipelines (Reno, Abilene) could outpace some primary metros in 2026 1. The falsifiers that would confirm the bull — and flip this primer’s “not-yet-at-the-edge” verdict — are concrete and watchable: (1) a non-scale vendor printing a genuine hyperscaler or marquee-neocloud DCI win (today: zero); (2) an independent analyst house (Dell’Oro, Cignal AI, LightCounting) publishing a standalone edge/regional-inference optical line item (today: none exists); (3) WDM Metro returning to sustained year-over-year growth after its seven-quarter decline 6; and (4) a single primary source attributing a specific metro-DWDM order to edge inference rather than campus DCI or generic capacity (the central evidentiary hole — even the cleanest tier-2/3 example, TKRZ, does not name AI as its driver 33). Until one of those prints, the weight of evidence sits with the tiered, not-yet-at-the-edge verdict.
